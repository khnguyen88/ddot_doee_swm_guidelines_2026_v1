# MEP, PROW Reconstruction, and Existing vs. Proposed SWRv
## Sourced from DOEE Stormwater Management Guidebook (January 2020) and DDOT Green Infrastructure Standards (2014)

---

## 1. What Does MEP Mean in Terms of SWM Sizing?

### 1.1 Regulatory Origin of MEP

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.1, p. B-1**

The term **Maximum Extent Practicable (MEP)** originates in the **Clean Water Act (CWA) Section 402(p)**, which authorized the National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System (NPDES) MS4 permit program. Congress did not require MS4 operators to achieve zero discharge or a fixed numeric standard — instead it required control of pollutants "to the maximum extent practicable." DC has applied this federal language directly to capital reconstruction projects in the Public Right-of-Way.

> "PROW reconstruction projects are required to address stormwater management (SWM) through the Maximum Extent Practicable (MEP) process." *(Appendix B.1, p. B-1)*

### 1.2 MEP as Applied to PROW Projects

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Chapter 2 (p. 7); Appendix B.1 (p. B-1); Table 2-2 (p. 10)**

MEP is the compliance framework applied **exclusively to reconstruction of existing Public Right-of-Way (PROW)**. Because full on-site retention is often physically impossible in constrained ROW conditions, MEP requires designers to retain as much stormwater as is physically and financially feasible — but does not require 100% of the retention target.

| Feature | MEP (PROW) | Full SWRv (Non-PROW) |
|---|---|---|
| **Retention target** | 1.2" (90th percentile) — same target | 1.2" (major land-disturbing) |
| **Compliance required** | To the maximum extent practicable | 100% of calculated SWRv |
| **SRC purchase if shortfall** | **Exempt** — no SRCs required for MEP shortfall | Required for off-site portion |
| **In-lieu fee if shortfall** | **Exempt** | Required if SRCs unavailable |
| **2-yr / 15-yr detention** | **Exempt** | Required for major land-disturbing |
| **Documentation** | MEP justification required at 65% and 90% design | Standard SWM Plan |

> "PROW Reconstruction (MEP): P = 1.2 inches to the maximum extent practicable." *(Table 2-2, p. 10)*

The critical distinction: a PROW project that achieves less than the full 1.2" SWRv due to physical constraints has satisfied its regulatory obligation under MEP — it is **not required to purchase SRCs or pay in-lieu fees** to cover the remainder.

### 1.3 MEP Site Assessment — 13 Factors

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.4.1–B.4.2, pp. B-7 to B-9**

Before determining what retention volume is achievable, the designer must conduct a site assessment evaluating 13 factors that influence GI/LID feasibility:

1. Soil type and infiltration rate
2. Depth to bedrock
3. Depth to seasonal high water table
4. Presence of underground utilities (gas, electric, water, sewer, telecom)
5. Existing trees and root zones
6. Traffic load and pedestrian volumes
7. Roadway geometry and grades
8. Available sidewalk width and buffer zones
9. Drainage patterns and existing inlets
10. Right-of-way width and obstructions
11. Proximity to buildings and foundations
12. Contaminated soils
13. Long-term maintenance access constraints

Each factor must be documented in the MEP justification. Infeasibility must be demonstrated location-by-location — a blanket assertion that BMPs are infeasible is insufficient.

### 1.4 Fundamental Tenets of MEP for PROW

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.4.3, p. B-10**

The guidebook lists 10 fundamental tenets that define what MEP requires designers to consider:

1. Select BMPs based on site-specific opportunities and constraints.
2. Size BMPs opportunistically — capture as much volume as site conditions allow.
3. Prioritize roadway runoff capture over sidewalk-only capture.
4. Minimize street width where feasible (reduces impervious area, increases available green space).
5. Maximize tree canopy (canopy interception and root zone infiltration count toward SWRv).
6. Use porous pavement where pavement condition and traffic allow.
7. Integrate traffic calming features (curb extensions, bump-outs) with bioretention.
8. Reduce impervious cover where feasible (e.g., gravel shoulders vs. paved shoulders).
9. Employ impervious surface disconnection (route runoff from impervious areas to permeable zones).
10. Document all considered and rejected options with justification.

### 1.5 MEP Design Process — 5 Steps

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.5, pp. B-12 to B-13**

| Step | Description |
|---|---|
| **Step 1** | Identify Site Drainage Areas (SDAs) and calculate SWRv for each using the 1.2" target and Equation 2.1 |
| **Step 2** | Evaluate infiltration feasibility — conduct soil borings or percolation tests; determine if native infiltration is viable |
| **Step 3** | Demonstrate consideration of existing infrastructure — identify utility conflicts, evaluate each candidate BMP location |
| **Step 4** | Apply land cover conversions and BMP placement — convert impervious to permeable where possible; locate bioretention, swales, tree boxes |
| **Step 5** | Size BMPs — calculate achievable retention volume from feasible BMPs; compare to SWRv target; document MEP shortfall if any |

### 1.6 MEP Design Submission Schedule

**Source: DDOT Green Infrastructure Standards (2014), Section 2.3.11.3, p. D-8; Section 2.4.3.37, p. D-9**

| Design Milestone | Required MEP Deliverables |
|---|---|
| **30% Design** | Preliminary site analysis; candidate GI/LID locations identified |
| **65% Design** | SWM Plan; BMP design submittals; MEP justification documentation |
| **90% Design** | Updated construction plans; updated MEP calculations |
| **100% Design (Final)** | Final SWM Plan; final BMP designs; as-built readiness confirmation |

### 1.7 Design Modifications Allowed Under MEP

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.4.5, p. B-11**

Standard BMP design rules are relaxed under MEP to accommodate ROW constraints:

| BMP Type | Standard Requirement | MEP Modification Allowed |
|---|---|---|
| **Sidewalk permeable pavement** | Underdrain typically required | Underdrain may be **omitted**; standard credit = **5.0 cf per 100 sf** of surface area |
| **Bioretention** | Open underdrain standard | **Capped underdrain** allowed; retention credit = **60% of total storage volume** |

These modifications recognize that full infiltration-based design is often impossible in urban ROW due to soil contamination, utility conflicts, and high groundwater.

### 1.8 MEP in DDOT GI Standards

**Source: DDOT Green Infrastructure Standards (2014), Sections 33.14.4.3, 33.14.4.4, 33.14.5.5, pp. D-12 to D-18**

The DDOT GI Standards reinforce MEP in BMP-specific sections:

- **Permeable pavement**: "Base/reservoir layer sized for 1.2-inch retention volume to the maximum extent practicable." *(Section 33.14.4.3, p. D-12)*
- **Bioretention**: "Sized to meet retention requirements to the maximum extent practicable." *(Section 33.14.5.5, p. D-18)*
- **MEP evaluation**: "The MEP process requires the designer to evaluate the physical constraints of the ROW and propose GI/LID practices to the maximum extent that site conditions allow." *(Section 33.14.4.4, p. D-13)*

## 2. SWRv: Existing vs. Proposed Conditions — Is There a Reduction?

### 2.1 SWRv Is Calculated on Post-Development Conditions Only

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Section 2.3, p. 10**

DC calculates SWRv based **entirely on post-development (proposed) conditions**. There is **no delta calculation** and **no credit for pre-existing impervious cover**. The regulatory text is explicit:

> "The regulated project site must employ BMPs and post-development land cover as is necessary to achieve the Stormwater Retention Volume (SWRv) that equals the post-development runoff from the applicable rainfall event." *(Section 2.3, p. 10)*

The SWRv formula (Equation 2.1, p. 10) uses I, C, and N values representing the **proposed** surface cover areas — not the difference between existing and proposed:

```
SWRv = P × [(RvI × I) + (RvC × C) + (RvN × N)] × 7.48 / 12
```

Where I, C, and N are the proposed impervious, compacted, and natural areas after construction.

### 2.2 How "Reduction" Actually Works for Existing/Improvement Sites

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Table 2-2 (p. 10); Sections 2.6 and 2.7 (pp. 20–22)**

The regulatory relief for existing/improvement projects does **not** come from a reduced SWRv calculation — it comes from two other mechanisms:

**Mechanism 1 — Lower Rainfall Depth (P value):**

| Activity Type | P Value | Reduction vs. Major Land-Disturbing |
|---|---|---|
| Major Land-Disturbing (non-AWDZ) | **1.2 inches** | Baseline |
| Major Substantial Improvement (non-AWDZ) | **0.8 inches** | **33% reduction** in P → ~33% less SWRv |
| Major Substantial Improvement (AWDZ) | **1.0 inches** | **17% reduction** in P → ~17% less SWRv |

For a site with identical surface cover areas, a Major Substantial Improvement project retains 33% less stormwater than a Major Land-Disturbing project in the same non-AWDZ location — solely because of the lower P value.

**Mechanism 2 — Exemption from Storm Detention Requirements:**

| Storm Control Requirement | Major Land-Disturbing | Major Substantial Improvement | PROW Reconstruction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2-Year Storm Detention | **Required** | **Exempt** *(Section 2.6, p. 20)* | **Exempt** *(Section 2.6, p. 20)* |
| 15-Year Storm Detention | **Required** (if triggered) | **Exempt** *(Section 2.7, p. 22)* | **Exempt** *(Section 2.7, p. 22)* |
| 100-Year Flood | If SFHA/building flood | If SFHA/building flood | If SFHA/building flood |

These detention exemptions represent significant cost savings for improvement and PROW projects — sizing and constructing detention infrastructure for major storms is often the most expensive element of stormwater compliance on new development.

### 2.3 Summary: What Changes and What Does Not

| Parameter | Existing/Improvement Sites | New Development |
|---|---|---|
| **SWRv formula** | Same formula | Same formula |
| **Existing impervious area credited** | **No — zero credit** | N/A |
| **P (rainfall depth)** | Lower (0.8" or 1.0") | 1.2" |
| **2-yr detention** | Exempt | Required |
| **15-yr detention** | Exempt | Required (if triggered) |
| **On-site minimum (MS4)** | 50% of SWRv | 50% of SWRv |

**Practical takeaway:** A project adding 5,000 sf of impervious pavement over an existing 5,000 sf of impervious parking lot pays the full SWRv on the 10,000 sf total proposed impervious area — there is no "net new impervious" concept in DC's SWRv calculation.

## 3. What Is Considered PROW Reconstruction or Improvement?

### 3.1 Definition of PROW

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.2, p. B-1**

The DOEE Guidebook defines the **Public Right-of-Way (PROW)** as:

> "The surface, air space above the surface, and area below the surface of any public street, bridge, tunnel, highway, railway track, lane, path, alley, sidewalk, or boulevard, including any easements or other property interests, that the District has the right to use for transportation or related purposes." *(Appendix B.2, p. B-1)*

In practical terms, PROW includes the full ROW width from property line to property line, encompassing:
- Travel lanes and roadway surface
- Curb and gutter
- Sidewalks and pedestrian zones
- Tree boxes and planting strips
- Alleyways
- Subsurface utility corridors within the ROW

### 3.2 What Activities Constitute PROW Reconstruction?

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.2, pp. B-1 to B-3**

PROW reconstruction for MEP purposes means **reconstruction of existing transportation infrastructure** for the purpose of its **operation and maintenance**. Qualifying project types include:

| Activity | Qualifies as PROW Reconstruction? |
|---|---|
| Full roadway reconstruction (curb-to-curb) | **Yes** |
| Full alley reconstruction | **Yes** |
| Sidewalk reconstruction | **Yes** (as part of broader PROW scope) |
| Bridge deck reconstruction | **Yes** |
| Lane widening or lane reconfiguration | **Yes** |
| Intersection reconstruction | **Yes** |
| Streetscape reconstruction (curb, sidewalk, planting strip) | **Yes** |
| Highway ramp reconstruction | **Yes** |
| Private subdivision road reconstruction | **No** — private roads are NOT PROW |

### 3.3 Two Types of PROW Projects

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.2, pp. B-2 to B-3**

The guidebook distinguishes two types of regulated PROW projects, each with different MEP obligations:

#### Type 1 — Sole PROW Reconstruction

A project that consists **solely of PROW reconstruction** with no associated parcel development.

*Examples:*
- DDOT reconstructing multiple blocks of a street corridor
- DC Water reconstructing an alley for utility replacement
- DDOT reconstructing a bridge

*MEP obligation:* The entire reconstructed PROW area is the regulated site. Retain to MEP within the LOD (limits of disturbance).

#### Type 2 — Parcel Project with Adjacent PROW Reconstruction

A parcel-based development project (new building, renovation) that **also reconstructs adjacent existing PROW** as part of the project scope.

*Examples:*
- Developer constructing a new building and reconstructing the sidewalk and curb in front
- DC government constructing a new school and reconstructing the adjacent street to install curb cuts

*MEP obligation:* The PROW reconstruction component is evaluated separately under MEP. The parcel itself is regulated under its applicable SWRv standard (major land-disturbing or major substantial improvement).

### 3.4 What Is NOT Considered PROW Reconstruction

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.4.4, p. B-10**

The following activities within or adjacent to PROW are **minor disturbances** that are treated differently under MEP:

| Minor Activity | MEP Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Utility trench installation or repair** — without full curb-to-curb reconstruction | **Exempt from MEP** if the trench is isolated and does not reconstruct the full roadway cross-section |
| **Driveway-only disturbance** | No exemption, but MEP opportunity is inherently limited; document limited BMP options |
| **Pavement patching or pothole repair** | Below threshold; not a regulated reconstruction activity |
| **Stripe repainting or signal replacement** | Below threshold; not a land-disturbing activity |

The key distinction for utility work: if the project reconstructs curb-to-curb (full cross-section), it is subject to MEP regardless of the reason for reconstruction. If the trench is a narrow utility cut with surface restoration only, it is typically treated as a minor disturbance.

### 3.5 Roadway Classification and MEP

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.3, p. B-5**

The guidebook includes a roadway classification table that links PROW type to design standards and MEP BMP applicability:

| Roadway Class | Examples | Typical MEP BMPs |
|---|---|---|
| Principal arterial | Major multi-lane streets | Permeable parking lanes, bioretention median/curb extension |
| Minor arterial | Secondary through roads | Curb extensions with bioretention, permeable sidewalks |
| Collector | Neighborhood through streets | Bioretention bump-outs, tree boxes, permeable pavement |
| Local (residential) | Local access streets | Bioretention curb extensions, roadside swales, permeable sidewalks |
| Alley | Service alleys | Permeable alley pavement, curb inlet filters |

Lower-volume local and residential streets generally have more flexibility for MEP BMP integration because they carry lower traffic loads, have lower pedestrian volumes, and have more available planting strip width.

### 3.6 Design Example: PROW MEP (from DOEE Guidebook)

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix A, Design Example 4, p. A-21**

The guidebook includes a worked PROW MEP example at the Green St. and Gold St. intersection:

- **Site area within LOD:** 13,528 sq ft (4 separate SDAs)
- **Total SWRv target (1.2" × proposed impervious):** 9,517 gallons
- **Achieved retention:** 7,160 gallons via bioretention at 3 of 4 corners
- **SDA 4:** Zero achievable BMP area — documented as infeasible; no SRC purchase required
- **MEP compliance:** Satisfied by demonstrating maximum feasible installation at each SDA and documenting why SDA 4 could not be improved

This example illustrates that MEP compliance does not require 100% achievement — it requires maximum feasible achievement with documentation of infeasibility where retention falls short.

## 4. Reducing Impervious Area in Proposed Design — Effect on SWRv and MEP

### 4.1 How Impervious Reduction Directly Reduces SWRv

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Section 2.3, p. 10; Equation 2.1, p. 10**

Because SWRv is calculated entirely on **proposed post-development conditions**, reducing impervious area in the proposed design automatically and directly reduces the SWRv requirement. There is no separate credit mechanism — the reduction is built into the formula:

```
SWRv = P × [(RvI × I) + (RvC × C) + (RvN × N)] × 7.48 / 12
```

Fewer proposed impervious square feet (`I`) means a smaller SWRv — less retention capacity required.

### 4.2 Runoff Coefficient Impact by Conversion Type

The regulatory benefit depends on what the impervious area is converted to:

| Conversion | Existing Coefficient | Proposed Coefficient | Effect on SWRv |
|---|---|---|---|
| Impervious → Natural/Landscaped | RvI = 0.95 | RvN = 0.00 | **Full reduction** — those SF contribute zero to SWRv calculation |
| Impervious → Compacted (gravel, etc.) | RvI = 0.95 | RvC = 0.25 | **Partial reduction** — 74% less runoff coefficient for those SF |
| Impervious → Permeable Pavement (BMP) | RvI = 0.95 | RvI = 0.95 (BMP surfaces stay in I) | **No reduction in SWRv target** — but the BMP provides retention credit toward meeting it |

The critical distinction with permeable pavement: BMP surfaces carry the same runoff coefficient as impervious surfaces (RvI = 0.95) for purposes of calculating the SWRv target. Converting impervious to permeable pavement does not shrink the target, but the permeable pavement itself counts as a retention BMP in the compliance calculation.

### 4.3 Numerical Example

Site: 5,000 sf proposed area, P = 1.2", MS4 area.

| Proposed Design | I (sf) | N (sf) | SWRv |
|---|---|---|---|
| All impervious — no reduction | 5,000 | 0 | 1.2 × (0.95 × 5,000) × 7.48/12 = **3,556 gal** |
| 1,000 sf converted to landscaping | 4,000 | 1,000 | 1.2 × (0.95 × 4,000) × 7.48/12 = **2,845 gal** |
| 2,000 sf converted to landscaping | 3,000 | 2,000 | 1.2 × (0.95 × 3,000) × 7.48/12 = **2,134 gal** |

Each 1,000 sf converted from impervious to natural removes approximately **711 gallons** from the SWRv requirement.

### 4.4 Impervious Reduction as an Explicit MEP Strategy

**Source: DOEE Guidebook, Appendix B.4.3, p. B-10; Appendix B.5, p. B-12**

For PROW projects, reducing proposed impervious area is not just a byproduct of good design — it is a codified MEP requirement. Two of the 10 MEP fundamental tenets directly address this:

> **"Reduce impervious cover where feasible"** — shrink the SWRv target numerically by converting impervious to natural cover in the proposed design. *(Appendix B.4.3, p. B-10)*

> **"Employ impervious surface disconnection"** — route runoff from impervious areas to permeable zones; reduces effective impervious contribution even where total impervious area cannot be reduced. *(Appendix B.4.3, p. B-10)*

The **5-step MEP design process** (Appendix B.5, p. B-12) formalizes this sequence:

**Step 4 — Land Cover Conversions and BMP Placement** is performed *before* BMP sizing. The designer first converts impervious to natural or permeable where feasible, reducing the SWRv target, and then sizes BMPs to meet the reduced target. This sequencing is intentional: reduce the requirement first, then meet it.

### 4.5 Practical Application in PROW Reconstruction

For street reconstruction projects, common impervious reduction strategies and their direct SWRv benefit:

| Strategy | How It Reduces Impervious | SWRv Benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Narrow travel lane width by 1–2 ft | Reduces paved roadway area (I) | ~0.71 gal SWRv reduction per sf removed at P=1.2" |
| Convert paved shoulder to grass shoulder | Moves shoulder area from I to N | Full runoff coefficient reduction (0.95 → 0.00) |
| Convert paved median to planted median | Moves median from I to N or C | Full or partial reduction depending on proposed cover |
| Widen planting strip at expense of sidewalk width | Reduces sidewalk I, increases N | Proportional to area converted |
| Convert paved alley to permeable alley | Stays in I category (BMP) | No SWRv target reduction, but full BMP credit |

### 4.6 What Impervious Reduction Does Not Do

There is **no "net new impervious" concept** in DC's SWRv calculation and no mechanism to bank reductions:

- If proposed impervious is less than existing but still greater than zero, the full SWRv is still owed on the proposed impervious area.
- A site that reduces from 10,000 sf to 7,000 sf of impervious owes SWRv on 7,000 sf — not on the 3,000 sf difference.
- Reducing below existing does not generate SRCs, offsets, or credits applicable to other sites or other regulatory requirements.
- There is no negative SWRv — converting the entire site to natural cover produces a SWRv of zero, not a credit.

The benefit is entirely internal to the project: a smaller proposed `I` value produces a smaller SWRv, which requires less BMP capacity to satisfy.
